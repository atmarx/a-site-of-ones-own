# Accessibility: A Site Everyone Can Read

Some of your visitors are blind and use software that reads pages aloud.  Some navigate entirely by keyboard.  Some are colorblind.  Some are reading on a cracked phone in direct sunlight on a moving bus.  Accessibility means your site works for all of them.

Public universities are required to make their websites accessible, but the better reason is simpler: these are your actual visitors, and the prospective student your site turns away might have been your best one.

## Your site checks itself

An accessibility checker is built into your editor.  While you work, it flags problems — an image with no description, a heading out of order — and explains each one in plain English, right there on the page.  Treat the flags like a spellchecker: not a suggestion box, just fix them as you go.  Most take seconds.

## The four habits that cover most of it

1. **Give every image alt text.**  Alt text is a one-sentence description of the image, spoken aloud by screen-reading software.  Write what matters about the picture: "Graduate student loading a sample into the mass spectrometer" — not "photo" or "IMG_4032."
2. **Use headings in order.**  Headings are the page's skeleton, and screen-reader users jump between them to navigate.  Pick heading levels by structure — Heading 2 for sections, Heading 3 for subsections — never by which size looks nice.  And never fake a heading by making a paragraph big and bold.
3. **Make links say where they go.**  "Read our recent paper on soil chemistry" works; "click here" does not — a visitor hearing only the links on a page gets "click here, click here, click here."
4. **Keep text readable against its background.**  Dark text on light backgrounds, or the reverse.  Pale gray on white looks refined and reads terribly.

## One trap to avoid: text inside images

A seminar flyer or poster uploaded as a single image looks fine — but every word in it is invisible to screen readers and to search engines alike.  Post the image if you like, then put the details (title, speaker, date, room) as real text beside it.

## The quiet bonus

Everything above also makes your site easier to skim, easier to find through search engines, and clearer on phones.  Accessible pages are simply better pages — you are not doing extra work, you are doing the same work properly.
