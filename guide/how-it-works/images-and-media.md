# Images and Photos

Nothing improves a lab website like good photographs, and nothing sinks one like clip art and empty gray placeholders.  The good news: the camera in your pocket is entirely good enough.

## What to photograph

- **People doing things.**  A student at an instrument beats a row of posed headshots.  Candid working shots are the single most effective content a lab site can have.
- **Hands and equipment.**  Close-ups of actual work — samples, screens, gloved hands, the machine with its lights on — photograph beautifully and say "active lab" louder than any paragraph.
- **Your space.**  Wide shots of the lab, the field site, the office whiteboard mid-argument.
- **The group.**  Take one group photo a year, every year.  Future-you will thank present-you.

## Practical details

- Use **JPEG** for photographs and **PNG** for diagrams, plots, and screenshots.  If you do not know which format a file is, look at the end of its filename.
- Upload the largest version you have.  The site automatically makes smaller copies for display, but it cannot invent detail that a tiny image never had.
- Prefer landscape (wider than tall) orientation — it fits page layouts better.
- Everything you upload lands in the **Media** library, a shared closet you can reuse.  Upload a photo once, use it on five pages.

## Whose photos can you use?

Not ones you found through an image search.  Photos on the internet are copyrighted by default, and universities regularly receive very real invoices from photographers' lawyers over an image someone grabbed for a departmental page.

Safe sources: your own camera, the university's official photo library, and free photo sites such as Unsplash or Pexels that explicitly permit reuse.  But honestly — a slightly imperfect photo of your actual lab beats a flawless stock photo of someone else's, every time.

## Photos of people

Ask before you publish a photo of someone, and take extra care with students.  A simple habit that solves this: when you take the yearly group photo, ask everyone right then whether they are fine appearing on the site.

## Describe every image

Every image needs **alt text** — a one-sentence description that screen-reading software speaks aloud for visitors who cannot see the image.  There is a field for it in the image's settings in the editor.  This matters enough to have [its own page](accessibility.md).
