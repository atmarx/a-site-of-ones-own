# The Research Page

The research page is the plain-language tour of what your group works on.  It is written for the curious non-expert — the prospective student, the potential collaborator from another field, the program officer skimming before a call.  The full technical detail already lives in your publications; this page is the map that makes someone want to go there.

## Organize by project or theme

List your three to six current projects or research themes.  If you have more than six, group them — a visitor confronted with fourteen projects reads none of them.

## For each project

- **A plain-language title.**  The project's grant title is usually not it.
- **One or two short paragraphs**: the question you are asking, why it matters, and how you go at it.  Written for the smart outsider — see [writing for visitors](../how-it-works/writing-for-the-web.md).
- **One image.**  A photo from the work, or a figure that a non-expert can take in without a paragraph of caption.
- **Who works on it**, linked to your [people page](people.md), if you like — students enjoy seeing their name on a project.
- **Funding acknowledgment.**  Many funders require one on public materials — for example, United States federal agencies such as the National Science Foundation generally expect the award number to appear.  Check your award's terms; when in doubt, a line like "This work is supported by [agency] under award [number]" covers it.
- **A link to related publications** for the reader who wants the real thing.

## Keep it honest and current

When a project ends, retire it — delete it, or move it to a short "Past projects" section.  A research page listing projects that wrapped up years ago tells every careful reader that the site cannot be trusted, and prospective students are very careful readers.

This page changes maybe twice a year.  That is fine.  It does not need to be busy; it needs to be true.
